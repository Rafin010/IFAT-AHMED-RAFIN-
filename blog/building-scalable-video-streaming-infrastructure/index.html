Building Scalable Video Streaming Infrastructure: Lessons From Origin Shielding to Edge CDNs

By Rafin • Systems Architect at X010 • Case Study: SportyXi

When engineers discuss web scale, they usually refer to REST APIs, SQL indexing, and database read replicas. However, distributed video distribution operates under radically distinct physics. In a video streaming network, a single viewer watching a 1080p60 stream pulls 4.5 Megabits of data every second.

Multiply that by 50,000 concurrent viewers and your cluster must sustain over 225 Gigabits per second of outbound traffic. In this architecture guide, we examine the systems design techniques we developed for SportyXi to distribute low-latency media without buckling under peak traffic loads.

1. The Four Tiers of High-Concurrency Video Delivery

A resilient video distribution network must decouple user request volume from transcoding compute. If end-users ever make direct requests to your ingest or encoding servers, your system will fail.

Architectural Isolation Tiers
  1. Ingest Gateway Tier: Go/SRT proxies receiving raw RTMP/SRT feeds from camera rigs.
  2. Transcode Cluster Tier: FFmpeg workers slicing uncompressed frames into 1s CMAF fragments.
  3. Origin Shield Tier: Multi-layered Nginx/OpenResty reverse proxies with single-flight mutex locking.
  4. Edge CDN Tier: Hundreds of edge PoPs serving cached chunks directly to client devices.

2. The Single-Flight Mutex: Eliminating Thundering Herds

In live Low-Latency HLS (LL-HLS), media manifests are constantly rolling. Every second, a new segment (`chunk-1049.m4s`) is produced. When an audience of 50,000 viewers reaches the edge of the playlist, they all request `chunk-1049.m4s` at the exact same millisecond.

If your CDN edges miss their cache, 50,000 requests cascade backward to your origin. This is the classic thundering herd.

To resolve this in SportyXi, we implemented an origin shield using Go and OpenResty. When request #1 arrives for `chunk-1049.m4s`, the shield locks a mutex key in memory and fetches the chunk from the transcode node. Requests #2 through #50,000 detect the active lock and wait asynchronously in memory. Once request #1 finishes downloading the byte stream, the shield broadcasts the buffered payload to all pending sockets.

Origin load stays at exactly 1 request per segment regardless of whether your audience is 50 viewers or 500,000 viewers.

3. Cache-Control Invariants: Playlists vs. Media Chunks

A fundamental mistake in streaming architecture is treating playlist manifests (`.m3u8`) and media chunks (`.m4s` / `.ts`) with identical caching strategies.

Asset Type Mutability Ideal Cache-Control Header
Live Playlist (`.m3u8`) Highly Mutable public, max-age=1, stale-while-revalidate=1
Media Chunk (`.m4s`) 100% Immutable public, max-age=31536000, immutable

4. Conclusion and Architectural Takeaways

Scaling video is fundamentally about predictability. By designing stateless transcode clusters, shielding origin tiers with mutex collapsing, and enforcing immutable chunk caching, platforms like SportyXi.com can stream global sports events with broadcast stability and sub-2.5 second latency.
